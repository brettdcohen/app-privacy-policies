The research behind Facet

Facet is a daily 3D-reasoning trainer for iPhone. This page says what the research shows, and what it does not.

Spatial skill can be trained

A meta-analysis of 217 studies (Uttal and colleagues, 2013, Psychological Bulletin) found that training improves spatial skill by about half a standard deviation. The gains lasted at delayed retests, and they carried over to spatial tasks that were not practised. Practice with feedback, course instruction and video games all helped. Read the abstract.

Slicing solids

Cohen and Hegarty trained people to picture the cut face of a solid, using interactive animations, and the training transferred to new objects that were not in the training. Cohen and Hegarty.

A course that works

Sorby's spatial visualization course (Michigan Tech) covers rotations, views, cross-sections and folding flat patterns. Students who took it did better afterwards, and it has been linked to students staying in engineering. Sorby; Sorby and Veurink.

Games

In one randomised study (Shute, Ventura and Ke, 2015), eight hours of Portal 2 improved spatial test scores and a brain-training product did not. Shute and colleagues. In another (Feng, Spence and Pratt, 2007), ten hours of an action game improved mental rotation. Feng and colleagues.

Variety matters

Practice on one kind of object mostly improves that kind of object (for example, Tetris and Tetris-like shapes; Sims and Mayer, 2002). Facet therefore mixes six different skills and always uses newly generated solids.

What we do not claim

Whether spatial training improves maths or general intelligence is contested, and some replications have failed (Cheng and Mix, 2014). Many of the studies have small samples. Facet does not promise anything beyond the spatial skills it trains, and it measures those directly with a fixed weekly Checkpoint. Facet is not a medical or diagnostic tool.
